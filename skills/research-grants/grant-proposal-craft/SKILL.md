# Grant Proposal Craft

How a proposal actually gets scored, and how to write to the scorer. Grounded in
a 2026 workshop for Indian researchers, but the mechanics are funder-neutral —
the same discipline applies to NIH, NSF, ERC, DST, or a foundation.

A proposal is a sales document and a methodological argument at the same time.
The reviewer's job is to form an opinion fast; every section should lower the
cost of that opinion in your favor.

## Hard rules

1. **No fabricated citations.** Every cited precedent must resolve to a
   verifiable record (DOI, PMID, arXiv ID, ISBN, URL with retrieval date). A
   gap in the literature that is a claim, not a verified absence, must be
   phrased as "as of this review" and the search logged.
2. **No claim without provenance.** Every quantitative claim — a budget line, a
   projected n, a timeline date, an acceptance figure — must point to a table,
   the OIM/circular, or a dataset the agent can show. "Trust me" is not
   provenance.
3. **No silent failure.** Every tool or lookup used to build a section (a
   literature search, an agency OIM read, a budget calculation) must report
   what happened on non-zero. A "we found no precedent" that came from a
   failed query is a false gap; say so.
4. **Title last.** The title is written after the body, when the point is
   settled. It must be clear, informative, and interesting without heavy
   jargon.
5. **Concrete beats plausible.** Every objective, method, and expected outcome
   is specific enough that a skeptic can test it. Vague aims are the most common
   reason a strong idea reads weak.

## When to use

- You are drafting the body of a research grant proposal and need the structure
  a reviewer actually scores (any funder).
- You are critiquing a draft and want a dimension-by-dimension pass (novelty /
  usefulness / methodology / who) with the specific gaps named.
- You need the lay-summary / abstract / title discipline, or a pitfall and
  budget realism check before submission.

## The four scoring dimensions

Most structured reviews (and every workshop rubric) score four independent
axes. Write to all four; a proposal that nails novelty but ignores *who* loses
to one that is merely competent across the board.

1. **Novelty** — is there something new? A funder wants to fund a gap, not a
   rerun. Frame the gap concretely, and if the closest prior work is nearly
   identical, do not apply — reframe or pick another scheme.
2. **Usefulness / relevance** — what does it buy the field or society?
   Stated plainly, in terms the reviewer's discipline values.
3. **Methodology** — is the plan doable? Methods are specific, and each
   anticipated failure has a named contingency (a "pitfall plan").
4. **Who (PI fit)** — is this the person to do it? A track record + a genuine
   reason this PI, not the next equivalent one, is the right owner.

The why/how/who spine: **why** now (the gap + its value), **how** (methods +
pitfalls), **who** (the PI's fit). A good proposal answers all three before the
reviewer asks.

## Writing the sections

- **Title** — last. Clear + informative + interesting, no jargon. Test: can a
  smart non-specialist tell what the project is from the title?
- **Abstract / synopsis** — the movie trailer, not the credits. It must make
  the *point* land in the first two minutes: gap → what you'll do → why it
  matters. Lead with the point, not the background.
- **Lay summary** — "the parent test": write it so a parent with no training
  in the field understands the problem and why it's worth doing. Zero jargon,
  no acronyms unexplained.
- **Project description** — objectives first, then rationale, primary data,
  methods, expected outcome.
  - Objectives: **SMART** — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant,
    time-boxed. Each maps to a deliverable.
  - Rationale: the concrete gap (sourced), not a survey of the field.
  - Methods: the specific approach, and — the signature of a strong proposal —
    the anticipated pitfalls and the named remedy for each.
- **Anticipated pitfalls** — write them yourself, before the reviewer does.
  "If X fails, we fall back to Y because Z." A proactively managed risk reads
  as confidence, not as doubt.
- **Deliverables & timeline** — a Gantt chart or table aligning each objective
  to a date and a concrete output (a dataset, a model, a paper, a pilot).
  Objectives without dated deliverables are not fundable.
- **Budget** — split non-recurring vs recurring; stay inside the agency's OIM /
  circular caps on personnel, overhead, and contingency. Realism is scored — an
  inflated or infeasible budget flags the whole proposal.
- **Supporting documents** — letters of support, an AI/plagiarism declaration
  where required, resource list, CV, and a publication list. Assemble early;
  they gate the submission.

## Specificity discipline

The single most common mistake, per the workshop: a **vague aim + vague method +
vague outcome** makes an excellent-sounding project look unfundable. For every
claim ask "specific enough to test?" and answer in one clause.

> Propose a **better approach**, not an "alternative." Positioning your work as
> "we also did X" is a claim of mere addition; positioning it as "the existing
> method breaks in case C, and here is why our approach is the right fix" is a
> claim of necessity. Funders fund necessity.

## Pitfalls

- **Writing the title first** and dragging the whole proposal into jargon.
- **A background-lead abstract** that buries the point — the reviewer's first
  two minutes are the whole first impression.
- **Survey-style rationale** ("X is important, Y is important...") with no
  concrete gap.
- **No pitfall plan** — the reviewer finds the risks for you and docks the
  methodology score.
- **Deliverables without dates**, or objectives that no deliverable maps to.
- **A budget that ignores the OIM caps**, or that is not internally feasible.
- **"We also did X"** positioning instead of the better-approach framing.

## Validation

- Title appears last in your edit history and passes the "smart non-specialist"
  test.
- Abstract's first two minutes carry the point; lay summary passes the
  "parent test."
- Every objective is SMART and maps to a dated deliverable in the Gantt.
- Every anticipated failure has a named contingency.
- Budget sits inside the agency's OIM/circular caps, split recurring vs
  non-recurring, with each line traceable.
